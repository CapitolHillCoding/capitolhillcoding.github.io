How computers work

In, think, out. On and off.

Before any code, a look inside. Every computer takes something in, thinks, and sends something out, and deep down it is all tiny switches.

  • Sort the parts on Lu's shelf: what senses, what thinks, what remembers, what shows?
  • Follow one key press from finger to screen.
  • Flip switches worth 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 to make any number.
  • Light up a picture from rows of 1s and 0s.

Blocks

Steps, loops and choices.

Widget rolls along a path, one block at a time. A block limit makes a loop the easy way out, and puddles that move every day make an if the only way through.

  • Steps in order: step, step, turn left, step.
  • Loops: say it once, then say how many times.
  • If and else: one program that has to work on Monday's puddles and Tuesday's.
  • Every block is one line of Python, and a switch shows the program as code.

Bugs

Bonk! Now find out why.

Dev writes programs fast and finds the bugs later. At the park, his programs are waiting to be fixed: a wrong turn, a loop one short, two blocks in the wrong order.

  • Press Go and watch. The block that went wrong lights up.
  • Nothing is marked wrong in red, and there is nothing to lose. Widget says what happened, and you try again.
  • When typed code has a mistake, the message says what to do about it.

Widget: I do not know the word "pritn". Did you mean "print"?

Python

Then she types the real thing.

At the library the blocks come off. The same ideas, in a real coding language: print, boxes with names (variables), loops, if and else, and her own new words with def.

  • Code tiles under the editor let most of a program be tapped together. The keyboard comes up for her own words.
  • The line that is running lights up, so she can see the program go.
  • It is real Python syntax: what she writes here would run on any computer with Python.
for i in range(5): if widget.puddle_ahead(): widget.jump() else: widget.forward()

Movies and games

Write a movie. In code.

The last stop is the Movie Studio. Pick a scene, put actors in it, and tell them what to say and do. Her own movie plays at Movie Night, and then the Studio is hers.

  • Ten places, the whole cast, twenty props and animals, or any emoji.
  • Actors say their lines out loud, move, jump, spin, grow and hide.
  • Add a function called tapped and the movie turns into a game.
  • Six starters to change and make her own.
scene("night") w = actor("widget") c = actor("cat") w.say("Is that a cat?") c.say("Meow!") for i in range(4): w.jump() c.spin()

For grown-ups

Capitol Hill Coding was made by a parent in Seattle for their own young coder, as a sister game to Maple Leaf Math, Licton Springs Language, Cascade Cardboard Creator and Wallingford Writing. Here's what you can expect.

🧠 Ideas before syntax

Sequence, loops, conditions, binary and debugging all come first, with taps and blocks. Typing arrives once the ideas are already hers.

🐍 What "real Python" means

The app runs a kid-sized part of Python: variables, numbers, words, lists, if, loops and functions. It is real syntax, but not the whole language.

⌨️ Typing is optional at first

Code tiles cover most of every level. The keyboard is there when she wants it, and for her own words inside quote marks.

🗣️ Every line is spoken

Everything a character says is read aloud by your device's own voice. You can turn the voice or the music off.

⏳ No pressure

No timers, no lives, no scores to lose. A program that doesn't work yet is how every coder spends most of the day.

🔒 Private by design

No ads, accounts, analytics or tracking, and the app never goes online. Everything she writes is saved only on the device. Privacy policy

🏙️ A real neighborhood

Broadway, Volunteer Park and Cal Anderson Park are real public places. Every business, the school and every person in the game is made up.

💻 Then try it for real

The best outcome is a kid who wants more. Python is free for any computer, and what she learned here is where it starts.
